A content calendar only does its job if you actually use it. Most that fail aren't badly
designed — they're too complicated to keep updating, so they get abandoned by week two. The
version below is deliberately minimal.

## The five columns that matter

For each planned post, you really only need:

1. **Date** — when it goes out.
2. **Channel(s)** — which platform(s), since not every post needs to go everywhere.
3. **Topic / hook** — the one-line idea, not the finished copy.
4. **Format** — text, image, video, link — decided up front so you're not scrambling for an
   asset the day of.
5. **Status** — idea → drafted → scheduled → posted.

That's it. Due date, budget columns, approval workflows — add them if your team genuinely needs
them, but they're the reason most calendars stop getting updated.

## Planning a month in one sitting

Block an hour, and work backwards from anything you already know is happening — a launch, an
event, a recurring weekly post — then fill the gaps with topic ideas. You don't need finished
copy yet, just enough of a hook that future-you knows what the post was going to be about.

## Turning the plan into actual posts

A spreadsheet is a plan. It isn't a schedule until the posts are actually queued up somewhere
that will publish them without you remembering to. Two features make that gap smaller:

- **[Cross-posting](/blog/cross-posting-to-multiple-platforms)** — write a post once and send it
  to every channel column in your calendar at the same time, instead of retyping it per platform.
- **[Recurring posts](/blog/recurring-social-media-posts)** — anything with a fixed cadence
  (a weekly roundup, a daily prompt) only needs to be set up once with a daily, weekly, or
  monthly repeat instead of re-added to the calendar every month.

Once a week's worth of posts are drafted, [scheduling them](/blog/how-to-schedule-a-post) takes
a couple of minutes each — and unlike a spreadsheet, a scheduled post actually goes out without
anyone opening a tab to remember it's due.
